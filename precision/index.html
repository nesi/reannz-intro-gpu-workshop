    4. Which GPU: precision¶

    Lesson Objectives

    • Know which GPUs Mahuika has, and how to ask for each one
    • Decide whether your work needs double precision, and what that rules out
    • Know when lower precision is worth using for machine learning

    Questions

    • What GPUs can I ask for?
    • My job runs on all of them. Does it matter which one I choose?

    The GPUs available on Mahuika¶

    We have a number of GPUs available on Mahuika for scientific research. These GPUs, and how you ask for each of them, are below:

    GPU Per node Total on Mahuika Request
    NVIDIA L4 4 16 l4:1
    NVIDIA A100 40 GB 3 6 a100:1 + --constraint=a100_40gb
    NVIDIA A100 80 GB 4 16 a100:1 + --constraint=a100_80gb
    NVIDIA H100 2 8 h100:1
    NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 2 8 pro_6000:1

    Asking for a particular A100

    Both A100s have the same Slurm name, a100, so --gpus-per-node a100:1 on its own can land on either of them. Choose which with --constraint (Using GPUs):

    #SBATCH --gpus-per-node a100:1
#SBATCH --constraint    a100_40gb     # the 40 GB A100

    #SBATCH --gpus-per-node a100:1
#SBATCH --constraint    a100_80gb     # the 80 GB A100

    Leaving the constraint out is the same mistake as leaving out the type in your first GPU job: you get whichever is free, and runs you cannot compare — or 40 GB of memory when your job needed 80.

    Optional exercise: ask for each A100

    Ask for each A100 in turn, and watch the constraint choose between them. --wrap runs a single command as a job, with no script:

    cd ~/gpu-training/precision
query="nvidia-smi --query-gpu=name,memory.total --format=csv,noheader"
sbatch --gpus-per-node a100:1 --constraint a100_40gb --wrap "$query"
sbatch --gpus-per-node a100:1 --constraint a100_80gb --wrap "$query"

    Each job writes one line to its slurm-<JobID>.out.

    What you should see 
    NVIDIA A100-PCIE-40GB, 40960 MiB
NVIDIA A100-SXM4-80GB, 81920 MiB

    Both jobs asked for a100:1. The constraint is what decided that one got the 40 GB card and the other the 80 GB.

    Two questions decide the card¶

    1. Does your software use single or double precision? Get this wrong and the job runs perfectly correctly, just many times slower than it should. That is this chapter.
    2. Will your job fit in the card's memory? If it does not, the job cannot run there at all. That is the next chapter.

    Precision comes first, because it rules cards in or out. Memory then picks between what is left.

    Precision¶

    What is precision?

    Precision is how many significant figures a number is stored with.

    Why is precision important?

    1. For some calculations, having too few significant figures introduces errors that build up over many iterations. It only affects some kinds of calculation.
    2. Different GPUs run different precisions at very different speeds.

    There are two precision types to focus on:

    Format Also called Significant digits Bytes
    float32 single precision, fp32 ~7 4
    float64 double precision, fp64 ~16 8

    Which precision type does my software use?

    It depends on what your software was written for.

    • fp32 is generally used for machine learning and AI, as well as for molecular dynamics.
    • fp64 is generally used where a high level of precision is needed, such as many chemistry and engineering codes where small errors can build up.
    • Always check your software's documentation to find out which it uses.

    As a rough guide:

    Usually needs fp64 Usually fine in fp32
    Quantum chemistry (VASP, Gaussian, CP2K) Machine learning, training and inference
    Computational fluid dynamics Molecular dynamics (LAMMPS, GROMACS)
    Climate and ocean models Image and signal processing
    Iterative solvers, eigenvalue problems Most Monte Carlo work

    Why does it matter which card?

    Every GPU can do both kinds of calculation. What differs enormously is how fast. These are the calculation speeds (TFLOPS) of each card at REANNZ:

    GPU fp32 fp64
    NVIDIA L4 30.3 TFLOPS 0.5 TFLOPS
    NVIDIA A100 (40 GB and 80 GB) 19.5 TFLOPS 9.7 TFLOPS
    NVIDIA H100 60 TFLOPS 30 TFLOPS
    NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 120 TFLOPS 1.9 TFLOPS

    A TFLOPS figure means little on its own, but the comparisons between cards and between precisions do:

    1. For fp32 work, the order is RTX PRO 6000 > H100 > L4 > A100.

      If you run machine learning or other fp32 work, this is the order of cards to consider for speed. The L4 beats the A100 here, though its 24 GB of memory may be too small for your job — that is the next chapter.

    2. At fp64, the L4 and RTX PRO 6000 fall far behind.

      Each does fp64 at about a sixtieth of its own fp32 speed. Against an A100, the L4 is about twenty times slower at fp64 and the RTX PRO 6000 about five times, and the H100 is three times faster again. If your work needs double precision, your choice is the A100 or the H100, whatever else is true.

    Do not run double-precision jobs on the L4 or RTX PRO 6000

    An eight-hour double-precision job on an A100 would take about a week on an L4. You will not see an error message for this, so watch out.

    Some final pointers

    • If your software can use fp32, use it over fp64. fp32 calculations are generally much faster than fp64.
    • If your software performs calculations where small errors compound over millions of steps, you will need fp64.

    Lower precision for machine learning

    fp32 and fp64 are not the only choices. Machine learning frameworks can also compute in bf16 or fp16, two bytes per number, and all four of Mahuika's GPU models have tensor cores that run these many times faster than ordinary fp32 arithmetic. They also support TF32, which runs fp32 matrix maths on the tensor cores.

    In PyTorch, mixed precision is a block around your forward pass, and TF32 is one line:

    torch.set_float32_matmul_precision("high")     # TF32 for fp32 matrix maths

with torch.autocast(device_type="cuda", dtype=torch.bfloat16):
    loss = model(batch)

    Two bytes per number also means half the GPU memory, which can be the difference between fitting a card and not. Most training is fine in bf16, but check that your results are — and none of this is any use to software that needs fp64.

    Optional exercise: watch fp32 go wrong

    If you have time:

    cd ~/gpu-training/precision
module load PyTorch/2.13.0-foss-2026-Python-3.14.4-CUDA-13.2.1
python3 accuracy.py
python3 show_fleet.py

    accuracy.py shows fp32 losing accuracy in three ways you can check by hand, and show_fleet.py prints the published speeds of every card side by side.

    Keypoints

    • Five things to ask for, differing in memory and in double-precision speed, not simply in how fast they are.
    • The A100 comes in 40 GB and 80 GB. They are the same chip at the same speed, and share the Slurm name a100 — choose with --constraint=a100_40gb or --constraint=a100_80gb.
    • Name the type, and ask for one unless your software can use more.
    • fp64 work belongs on an A100 or H100. At fp64 the L4 is about twenty times slower than an A100, and the RTX PRO 6000 about five, with no warning.
    • For machine learning, bf16 and TF32 run on the tensor cores, faster than fp32 and in half the memory.
